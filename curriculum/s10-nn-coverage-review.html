1. Why ordinary code coverage doesn't map onto a neural network

Traditional structural coverage — statement, branch, or path coverage — measures how much of a program's explicitly written logic your tests exercised. A trained neural network doesn't have that kind of logic to walk through: there are no if/else branches representing its decisions. Its behavior is entirely dictated by learned weights, biases, and the activation values they produce — the same computation you built by hand in Session 9, just repeated across possibly millions of neurons.

That gap is why a separate family of neural network structural coverage metrics has emerged: measures designed to describe how thoroughly your test inputs have exercised a model's internal mechanisms, even though there's no source code to point at.

⚠
Scope check This is specifically about the trained model's internals. Ordinary code coverage is still entirely relevant to the code around the model — the data pipeline, pre/post-processing, and the application logic that calls the model. It's only the model itself that needs a different lens.

2. Neuron Coverage

The most basic of the three: Neuron Coverage measures the proportion of neurons in the network whose output (activation value) exceeded a specified threshold at least once, across all the test inputs you ran.

Think of it like a coverage map for a huge control panel of switches: running tests tells you which switches actually got flipped "on" at least once. If a neuron's activation value never rises above the threshold during testing, you have no evidence at all about how that neuron behaves once it does activate — it's untested territory.

3. k-Multisection Neuron Coverage (kMNC)

Plain neuron coverage has a blind spot: a neuron could be marked "covered" the moment it crosses the threshold once, even if every test input only ever pushed it into one narrow slice of its full output range. k-Multisection Neuron Coverage fixes this by dividing each neuron's known output range into k equal sections (buckets), then measuring the proportion of all those buckets, across all neurons, that were activated by at least one test input. It's a finer-grained lens on the same idea — not just "did this neuron ever fire," but "how much of its actual behavioral range did we exercise."

4. Neuron Boundary Coverage (NBC)

Neuron Boundary Coverage measures something different: the proportion of neurons whose output during testing went beyond the range that neuron ever produced during training — either higher than its training-time maximum, or lower than its training-time minimum.

These out-of-range activations matter because they mark exactly the inputs the network never experienced while learning — the corner cases where its behavior is least predictable and most likely to expose a defect or a fragile, unlearned response.

6. The catch: structural coverage alone isn't enough

High structural coverage tells you which parts of the network you've exercised — it does not tell you the network is right. Neural networks can learn spurious correlations, producing activations that look perfectly normal while relying on the wrong reasons entirely. Commercial tool support for these specific coverage measures is also still limited today. That's why testers pair structural coverage with other techniques rather than relying on it alone — adversarial testing and metamorphic testing, which you'll build hands-on in Sessions 18 and 19, are exactly the complementary tools for catching "right activation, wrong reason" failures that coverage numbers can't see.
