1. Locked vs. adaptive: two very different testing problems

Back in Session 1 you saw that an AI-based system's behavior can keep changing after release, and that this is one of the assumptions traditional testing quietly relies on and AI breaks. Chapter 4 gives that idea a name and a hard line down the middle: every AI-based system is, at any given moment, either locked or adaptive.

Locked AI-based system

Behavior is fixed once deployed. A trained DNN's weights and biases don't change in production — the only way to change what it does is to retrain it and ship a new version. Safety-critical components like a self-driving car's lane-detection or traffic-sign-recognition model are typically locked on purpose: predictable behavior is the whole point.

Adaptive AI-based system

Behavior can change after deployment — most classically a reinforcement-learning system updating from a reward signal, or a system retraining itself against a shifting operational environment. An e-commerce recommender that keeps adjusting to a shopper's evolving behavior is adaptive. Crucially, the specific direction of that change can't be predicted in advance.

Most GenAI systems, like LLM-based chatbots, sit in between: they're locked at runtime (the deployed model doesn't change mid-conversation) but get periodically retrained or fine-tuned and re-released — so in practice, AI-based systems span a continuum from fully deterministic and locked to deliberately self-learning and adaptive, rather than falling neatly into two boxes.

DimensionLockedAdaptive
Behavior after deploymentFixed until a new version is retrained and shippedCan change on its own, e.g. via a reward function or new operational data
TestabilityLargely deterministic — expected results stay valid over timeBehavior can change during or because of testing itself
What "re-test" meansAn updated locked model is effectively treated as a new system — full retestYou can't pre-write test cases for behaviors that don't exist yet

⚠
"Locked" doesn't mean "perfectly deterministic" Even a locked, deployed DNN can behave non-deterministically in practice — floating-point precision limits and parallel execution on GPU hardware can produce tiny run-to-run differences. "Locked" describes the model's parameters, not a guarantee of bit-for-bit repeatable output.

2. Why pass/fail thresholds replace exact-match assertions

A conventional test asserts actual == expected. That single-instance thinking breaks down for AI-based systems, and the syllabus gives four concrete reasons a statistical approach is usually required instead:

Non-determinism

The same input may not always produce the same output. One instance of a model mislabeling a cat as a dog doesn't tell you the model is broken — or working — you need a large enough test suite for the result to be statistically meaningful.

Distributional performance

Training data never perfectly matches the real operational environment. You need a statistically representative sample of real-world scenarios to know how the model will actually behave, not just how it behaves on hand-picked examples.

Uncertainty and bias

AI systems can be confidently wrong. Statistical tools — confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, error analysis — let you quantify accuracy, fairness, and robustness instead of eyeballing a handful of examples.

Regulatory & safety context

Regulated domains (healthcare, transport) often require demonstrating that a system meets a safety or fairness bar with high confidence across a broad range of scenarios — a claim only statistical evidence can support.

The practical upshot: instead of "did it get the exact right answer," AI testing usually asks "does its rate of correct, safe, or acceptable behavior clear a defined threshold, measured across a large enough and representative enough sample?" You'll get hands-on with the statistical machinery behind this — confidence intervals, sampling, significance — when you reach the Model Testing sessions (17–20).

3. The oracle problem, in depth

A test oracle is whatever mechanism tells you whether a test passed or failed — usually, the expected result you compare the actual result against. In conventional software, a clear specification usually makes the oracle obvious: the requirements document is the oracle. AI-based systems break this in five distinct ways:

  • Probabilistic & non-deterministic nature — outputs can vary for identical inputs, so a strict pass/fail check often needs a threshold or tolerance range instead of one exact value.
  • Exploratory development & incomplete specifications — AI development is often exploratory by nature; requirements may be vague, evolving, or missing entirely, leaving nothing precise to generate expected results from.
  • Complexity of tasks — some tasks are too complex for a human to verify by hand at all, making manual oracle checks impractical.
  • Subjectivity of behavior — "correct" can be a matter of opinion. What one user expects from a virtual assistant, another user disagrees with.
  • Self-learning systems — for an adaptive system, "correct" behavior can legitimately change over time as the model keeps learning, so a test oracle fixed at one point in time can go stale.
💡
Ground truth isn't always available When a task has an objective, verifiable answer, testers can use ground truth — a trusted, independently verified correct answer — as the oracle. The whole point of the oracle problem is that for a large share of real AI-based systems (open-ended GenAI output, subjective quality judgments, genuinely novel predictions) ground truth simply doesn't exist to check against.

The syllabus offers five overlapping strategies for building a usable oracle anyway:

StrategyHow it works
Output boundariesAgree on acceptable ranges, distributions, or tolerances instead of one exact value — e.g. an autonomous car must stop within a maximum distance.
Environmental boundariesPin down test-environment conditions (lighting, temperature, network latency) so the same conditions reliably produce comparable, repeatable output.
Expert consultationDomain experts judge expected results directly — useful, but their opinions can differ or simply be wrong.
Specialized testing techniquesA/B testing, back-to-back testing, and metamorphic testing compare behaviors or verify properties without needing an explicit expected output for every single case (you'll build a metamorphic-testing lab in Session 19).
Proxy oraclesUse a secondary system — sometimes another AI model — to judge the output when no direct expected result exists, e.g. training a separate model on labeled data to predict for unlabeled test cases. Use with care: a proxy oracle can reproduce the same biases or blind spots as the system it's checking.
