Evaluation Pipelines, Launch Gates & Drift Monitoring

Instrumentation and CI/CD gates (Session 23) tell you a change didn't break anything you already test for. Today's session is about the bigger machine those gates plug into: how a full evaluation pipeline is architected, what concrete criteria decide whether an AI feature is actually ready to launch, and how you keep watching it once it's live — because passing your evals once is not the same as staying correct.

1. Evaluation pipeline architecture

Every serious AI evaluation system, no matter how it's built, breaks down into the same four stages. Knowing them by name makes it much easier to diagnose why an eval result is misleading you — the problem is almost always in one specific stage.

1. Golden dataset

A curated, versioned set of representative inputs — common cases, known edge cases, and past production failures (fed straight from the reproducible traces built in Session 23). It must be refreshed as the product evolves; a stale golden dataset quietly stops reflecting reality even while every eval keeps "passing."

2. Scoring functions

The function(s) that turn one output into a number or label: exact-match, rule-based checks (regex, schema validation), reference-based similarity, model-graded ("LLM-as-judge" against a rubric), or human grading used as the gold standard to calibrate the automated scorers. Different quality dimensions — correctness, safety, tone, format — usually need different scoring functions.

3. Aggregation

How individual scored examples roll up into one reportable number: a simple mean, a pass-rate against a per-item threshold, a weighted average by segment importance, or a worst-case/minimum across critical segments. This choice matters enormously — a mean and a worst-segment aggregation can tell opposite stories about the same raw scores (this is Session 22's distributional thinking, applied at the pipeline-design level).

4. Reporting

The dashboard or report that surfaces the aggregated score, breakdowns by segment, trend over time, and drill-down into individual failing examples — built for the people who actually decide whether to ship, hold, or roll back (Session 25).

🧭
Diagnosing a misleading result "The eval score looks great but users are complaining" almost always traces back to one of these four stages: a golden dataset that no longer represents real traffic, a scoring function that's miscalibrated against what users actually care about, an aggregation that hides a failing segment behind a healthy mean, or a report nobody with the authority to act ever actually looks at.

2. Launch readiness gates

A launch readiness gate is a specific, checkable criterion that must be satisfied before an AI feature is allowed to ship — or to stay live. The most effective gates are defined before development finishes, not negotiated under pressure the week of launch, precisely so nobody can quietly move the goalposts when a deadline is close.

Gate categoryWhat it checks
Accuracy / quality floorThe blocking-metric threshold from the evaluation pipeline (Session 22's blocking metrics, measured via the pipeline above).
Safety checksToxicity/harmful-content rate, PII leakage, resistance to jailbreak or adversarial prompts (ties to red teaming, Session 12).
Latency budgetp95/p99 response time under a hard ceiling — the distributional view of latency from Session 22, not just the mean.
Cost ceilingCost per request within budget at expected volume.
Human review sign-offA qualified human — domain expert, legal/compliance, or product owner — reviews a representative sample and formally signs off, especially for regulated or high-stakes domains.
Rollback readinessA tested kill switch or fast-revert path exists in case something goes wrong after launch.

Not every feature needs every gate at full strength — an internal, low-stakes prototype and an externally-facing, regulated financial tool warrant very different rigor. What matters is that the gate list and thresholds are decided deliberately, scaled to actual risk, and agreed before testing starts.

3. Monitoring for drift once a system is in production

Clearing every launch gate answers "was this system good enough on the day it shipped?" It says nothing about next month. AI systems can silently degrade in production through drift — the same concept Session 19 covers from a testing-design angle, revisited here from the live-monitoring side:

  • Data drift — the real-world inputs the system sees start looking different from the data it was trained or validated on.
  • Concept drift — the relationship between inputs and the "correct" answer itself changes over time (user expectations shift, a policy changes, a new fraud pattern emerges).

Live monitoring means continuously recomputing a subset of your eval metrics — or fast proxy signals like confidence-score distribution, human-escalation rate, or refusal rate — against a sample of real production traffic, then comparing the result to a stored baseline. When a metric crosses a threshold or shows a statistically meaningful trend shift, an alert fires.

Canary / shadow evaluation

Before a full rollout, run the new version alongside the old one on a small slice of traffic (or in "shadow" mode, where its output is scored but never shown to users) to catch problems before they reach everyone.

Ongoing production monitoring

After full rollout, continuous sampling and scoring keeps watching indefinitely — this is what catches drift that only appears weeks or months after launch, well past the point any pre-launch gate could have seen it.

🔔
Design alerts deliberately An overly sensitive threshold trains people to ignore alerts ("alert fatigue"), which defeats the entire point of monitoring. Every alert should tie to a clear owner and a runbook (Session 25 covers who that owner is) — and any real failure this monitoring catches should feed back into the golden dataset, closing the loop with the pipeline built earlier in this session.
