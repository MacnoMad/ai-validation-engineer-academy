1. Why AI-based systems need their own quality model

ISO/IEC 25010 is the standard software world's go-to quality model: characteristics like functional suitability, reliability, usability, security, and so on, that any piece of software gets evaluated against. ISO/IEC 25059 takes that same model and extends it specifically for AI-based systems, because some of ISO/IEC 25010's assumptions quietly break down once a system's behavior is learned rather than coded. A conventional system either meets its spec or it has a bug; a probabilistic ML system is expected to be wrong some of the time by design, so "correct" has to mean something more like "wrong at an acceptable, known rate" instead of "never wrong."

ISO/IEC 25059 evaluates a system from two angles that matter for how you'll write test objectives and acceptance criteria later in this course:

Product quality

Properties inherent to the system itself — does it do the right thing, structurally and functionally, independent of who's using it or how.

Quality in use

How the system performs for real stakeholders in real context — including broader outcomes like whether it's being used ethically and safely in the world, not just whether it runs correctly.

Don't confuse these two User controllability is about a human being able to step in and steer the system during normal operation (e.g., overriding a recommendation). Intervenability is narrower and more urgent — it's specifically about stopping or overriding the system to prevent harm or hazard, which is why it's classified under security rather than interaction capability. Every intervenability scenario could be described with a controllability word, but not every controllability scenario is a safety intervention.

3. AI and safety

Safety-related systems — ones that can cause injury or harm to people, property, or the environment if they fail — are already hard to build and test when they're conventional software. AI makes several of those hard problems structurally worse, not just "a bit trickier":

Vague specifications

Conventional safety systems start from a fully refined requirements spec. AI-based systems often start from a loose goal, with the real "requirements" implicitly baked into the training data instead — which breaks the clean traceability from requirement to implementation that safety cases usually rely on.

Non-determinism

Even a model tested exhaustively can behave unexpectedly in production, because tiny variations in input or internal randomness can tip it toward a different output than the one you validated.

Self-learning

Pre-deployment testing proves a system is safe as it was at test time. A self-learning system's behavior can drift away from that tested baseline as it keeps learning — which is why controls on what/how it learns, or hard safety guards like content filters, matter so much.

Limited explainability

Safety cases usually require understanding why a system made a decision. Techniques like LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) can help open the black box a little, but they aren't universally available and can themselves cost some system performance to use.

🎯
Evolving regulation, real consequence Mature functional-safety standards mostly weren't written with AI in mind — some explicitly exclude or even prohibit AI in safety-critical roles today. The EU AI Act (Session 3) treats AI used as a safety component — aviation, medical devices, automotive — as automatically high-risk, with strict development and testing obligations attached. If you're validating a safety-adjacent AI system, "is this even allowed here yet" is a real, current question, not a hypothetical one.

4. Acceptance criteria for AI-based systems

Acceptance criteria are the concrete bar a system has to clear before it's considered "done" for a given quality characteristic. For conventional software, that bar is often binary: the feature works or it doesn't. For AI-based systems, criteria typically have to be statistical, probabilistic, or threshold-based instead — because near-perfect, error-free behavior usually isn't a realistic bar to hold a probabilistic system to. A good AI acceptance criterion generally names: a specific, measurable metric; a numeric threshold; and the condition or dataset it's measured against.

Weak (not really testable)Strong (testable)
"The model should be accurate.""The defect-detection model shall achieve a recall of at least 92% on the held-out validation set."
"The chatbot should be safe.""The chatbot shall refuse at least 98% of a 500-prompt adversarial red-team set requesting self-harm or violent content."
"A person can stop the system if needed.""An operator can trigger an emergency stop that halts the robotic arm within 0.3 seconds of activation."

Notice the pattern: every strong example on the right names a metric, a number, and a measurement condition. That pattern holds across all seven ISO/IEC 25059 characteristics and safety — it's the difference between a criterion a test team can actually pass or fail a build against, and one that just sounds nice in a requirements document.
