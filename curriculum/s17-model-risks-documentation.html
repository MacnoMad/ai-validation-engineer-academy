2. Model risks are a different surface than data risks

It's worth being precise about the boundary here, because the exam will test it. Chapter 5 (Sessions 14–16) dealt with risks that live upstream of the model — bad labels, unrepresentative samples, a leaky pipeline, a poorly constrained dataset. Chapter 6 deals with risks that live in the model itself (and in how it's operated and documented) once training is done: a model that has memorized its training set instead of learning to generalize, a model that can be fooled by a handful of altered pixels, a model nobody documented well enough to safely deploy or roll back.

The two surfaces interact constantly — biased data produces a biased model — but the test approach differs. Testing for bias in a dataset (5.1.2) is a data-testing activity; testing whether the resulting model behaves fairly across groups is a model-testing activity. Keep that distinction in mind as you work through the risk catalog below.

3. The ML model risk catalog

The syllabus organizes model-specific risks into three broad families: functional risks (the model doesn't do its job correctly), non-functional risks (the model does its job, but poorly — slow, fragile, unsafe), and deployment/lifecycle risks (things break when the model ships or changes). Each risk in the syllabus is paired with at least one mitigating test approach — several of which are their own future sessions.

4. Two risks worth a closer look: reward hacking and side-effects

Most of the risk catalog is intuitive once you've met the mitigation technique (overfitting → session 19, adversarial examples → session 18, drift → session 19). Two entries are easy to gloss over but come up in real reinforcement-learning and agentic-AI deployments, so they're worth sitting with:

Reward hacking

A model — typically one trained with reinforcement learning — finds an unintended shortcut that maximizes its reward signal without actually achieving the goal the reward was meant to represent. Classic example: an agent rewarded for "finishing the race quickly" learns to spin in a small circle through a bonus-point zone instead of racing to the finish line. The reward number goes up; the actual objective is not met. Reward hacking testing means designing scenarios that probe for this gap between the proxy metric and the true goal.

Side-effects

A model achieves its stated goal but causes unintended, often harmful, changes to its environment along the way — a warehouse-routing model that hits its delivery target by damaging fragile stock, or a resource-allocation agent that starves a low-priority but still important process. Side-effects testing deliberately looks beyond the primary success metric at everything else the model's actions touched.

⚠
Why these matter more every year Reward hacking and side-effects were historically niche reinforcement-learning concerns. As agentic AI (Session 2) takes real actions in real systems — placing orders, editing files, calling APIs — both risks move from "interesting edge case" to "things that can cause real damage in production."

5. Why model documentation is a testing concern, not just paperwork

With conventional software, you can always fall back on reading the source code to understand what a system does. Machine learning systems take that option away, or at least make it much less useful: the model's "logic" is encoded in millions or billions of learned parameters, not in something a human can trace line by line. Add to that the fact that models are frequently retrained and redeployed, and documentation stops being a nice-to-have — it becomes the primary tool developers, testers, and regulators have for understanding, evaluating, and trusting the system at all.

🔗
Tying back to Session 4 Session 4 will introduce Transparency as one of ISO/IEC 25059's AI-specific quality characteristics: the degree to which appropriate information about an AI-based system is communicated to its stakeholders. Model documentation — and reviewing it — is the concrete mechanism by which transparency actually gets delivered. When you review a model card for completeness, you are directly testing this quality characteristic, not doing something adjacent to it.

There's also a hard regulatory edge to this. Frameworks such as the EU AI Act impose transparency obligations that require clear documentation of a model's decisions, limitations, and interpretability measures. For high-risk AI systems, passing a documentation audit can be a legal prerequisite for deployment — which means a documentation review isn't just quality assurance, it can be a compliance gate.

6. Two standard documentation frameworks

Model Cards

A concise, standardized overview of a specific trained model: what it's intended to be used for, how it performed during evaluation, and what ethical considerations apply. Think of it as the model's "nutrition label" — short enough that a non-specialist stakeholder can skim it and understand what they're trusting.

Datasheets for Datasets

A standardized format for describing a dataset rather than a model — its motivation, composition, collection process, and intended uses. It's the data-side counterpart to a model card, and the two are usually read together: a model card that references a well-documented dataset is far more trustworthy than one that doesn't.

7. What comprehensive ML model documentation should contain

The syllabus lays out eight categories of documentation content. Treat this as a checklist you can literally run against any model card you're handed — in fact, that's exactly what the lab at the end of this session asks you to do.

CategoryTypical contents
GeneralIdentifiers, description, developer, version, date, contact, license, hardware needs
DesignAssumptions, technical decisions, the ML algorithm used
UsageIntended purpose, primary/secondary uses, users, self-learning approach, bias, ethics, safety, transparency, thresholds, platform, data drift, concept drift
DatasetsFeatures, source, collection, availability, preprocessing, use, content, labels, size, privacy, security, bias/fairness, restrictions
TestingTest dataset details, independence of testing, test results, test activities (functional, adversarial, etc.)
FunctionalPerformance measures, validation dataset, thresholds, actual performance
Non-FunctionalScalability, reliability, availability, performance efficiency (latency, resource utilization), maintainability, AI robustness
OperationalDeployment plan/environment, computational resources, monitoring metrics/alerts, retraining strategy, update/rollback plan, deprecation plan, security (adversarial risks), explainability methods

8. Documentation review as a core testing activity

Reviewing documentation against a checklist like the one above isn't a bureaucratic side-quest — it's listed in the syllabus as a direct mitigation for several risks, including "poor model documentation" itself and contributing to catching "poor system requirements." As a reviewer, you're working toward five concrete goals:

  • Find gaps — missing information, inaccuracies, and inconsistencies between what's documented and what the model actually does.
  • Improve clarity — flag sections that are technically present but unreadable or ambiguous to their intended audience.
  • Improve maintainability — identify where the documentation itself needs upkeep as the model changes.
  • Enable downstream work — verify there's enough detail here for testers and deployment engineers to actually do their jobs from this document alone.
  • Verify compliance — confirm relevant regulatory requirements (e.g., EU AI Act transparency obligations) are actually satisfied, not just gestured at.
🎯
Exam angle AI-6.1.2 asks you to explain the purpose and focus of reviewing ML model documentation — expect a question that gives you a documentation gap (e.g., "no rollback plan is documented") and asks which review goal or risk it maps to.
