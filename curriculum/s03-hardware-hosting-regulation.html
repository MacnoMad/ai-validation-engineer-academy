1. Hardware for machine learning systems

Every model has to run on physical silicon somewhere, and the hardware choice isn't cosmetic — it decides how fast a model trains, how much it costs to run, how much power it burns, and even where it can physically be deployed. Two different phases of a model's life often use two different kinds of hardware entirely: training (building the model from data — usually the heavier job) and inference (using the finished model to make a prediction — usually the lighter, repeated job). A speech-recognition model might be trained on a rack of datacenter GPUs but do its actual inference on the modest chip inside a budget smartphone.

What ML workloads generally want from hardware is fairly specific: the ability to hold and move around large data structures (weights, activations, batches of input), the ability to run huge numbers of identical, simple operations at the same time (this is what "massively parallel" means — think matrix multiplication, which is just millions of small multiply-and-add operations repeated over and over), and, increasingly, the option to trade numeric precision for speed via quantization — doing the math with fewer bits per number (say, 4 or 8 bits instead of 32) so each operation is cheaper, more of them fit in memory and cache at once, and the chip draws less power. The tradeoff is a small amount of accuracy lost to rounding error, which is usually an acceptable price for the speed and cost gained.

Common misconception A CPU isn't "worse" than a GPU in general — it usually runs at a higher clock speed and handles complex, branching logic better. 2. Development and hosting of AI models

Two separate decisions get made, often independently: where a model gets built (development) and where it runs once it's finished (hosting). Neither has one right answer — the correct choice depends on model size, budget, latency needs, and how sensitive the data is.

Local development

Coding and training on your own machines. Gives you full control and keeps data private. Fine for small models (decision trees, compact neural nets) on a laptop; mid-sized models need a dedicated GPU; large models need an on-premises server cluster — with the power, cooling, and hardware costs that implies.

Cloud development

Public cloud providers offer pre-configured environments, pay-as-you-go pricing, and easy scaling with little upfront hardware spend. Private clouds trade some of that convenience for stronger security and privacy guarantees, at a higher upfront cost.

Hybrid development

Very common in practice: prototype locally, then scale training out to the cloud; keep sensitive steps (like preparing private data) on-premises while renting cloud compute only for the heavy, non-sensitive lifting.

Build vs. buy

Third-party AI (pretrained models or "AI as a Service" — AIaaS) gets you to market fast with less specialized talent needed. Building privately gives you a system tailored exactly to your requirements, but demands in-house ML expertise or an outsourced team to get there.

Hosting mirrors the same three shapes. Local hosting runs the finished model on personal devices or on-premises servers — private and free of ongoing cloud licensing fees, but limited by whatever hardware is physically present. Cloud hosting (public or private) gives you elastic, maintenance-free scaling that's ideal for workloads that spike unpredictably, at the cost of ongoing fees and less direct control. Hybrid hosting runs some inference locally — often for latency or privacy reasons — while bursting to the cloud when demand or complexity requires it.

3. Machine learning development frameworks

An ML development framework is the toolkit developers build a model with, roughly the way a web framework is the toolkit a website gets built with. Rather than writing matrix math and gradient calculations by hand, developers work through a framework that already provides the hard parts:

  • Data handling — loading, cleaning, formatting, and transforming data into whatever shape the model needs.
  • Model building — libraries of algorithms and building blocks for specifying the model's architecture: what type of model, how many layers, how they connect, what math runs inside them.
  • Training and optimization — the actual algorithms that iteratively adjust the model's internal parameters against the training data, sometimes across many machines at once (distributed training), and sometimes starting from an existing pretrained model instead of from scratch.
  • Evaluation — built-in tooling to measure how well the trained model performs on data it hasn't seen — accuracy, precision, recall for classification; error rates for regression (you'll calculate these yourself in Session 7).
  • Deployment — converting the trained model into a format that can actually be dropped into a web app, mobile app, edge device, or embedded system.

Frameworks also differ in abstraction level — a lower-level API gives more control but demands more coding expertise; a higher-level API is faster to build with but offers fewer knobs to turn — and in specialization, from general-purpose frameworks that handle almost anything to narrow ones tuned for a single domain like speech or vision. When you're picking (or evaluating a team's choice of) a framework, the real decision factors are: the application area, how much rapid-prototyping friendliness you need versus configurability for complex models, the team's actual expertise, deployment constraints (some frameworks are much lighter-weight for resource-constrained edge devices), and the maturity of the community and ecosystem around it — mature ecosystems mean more pretrained models, more documentation, and fewer dead ends when something breaks.

💡
Why a tester should care about the framework at all The framework choice shapes what's testable and how. A framework with strong built-in evaluation tooling makes it far easier to get the metrics you need for a test report; a low-level framework used for a highly custom architecture might mean nothing is standard and every test harness is bespoke. Framework choice is a legitimate topic to raise in test planning, not just an engineering concern.

4. Regulations and standards for AI

Regulations and standards exist to build trust in AI and push development toward being safe, fair, transparent, accountable, and used responsibly — and, increasingly, they define what "compliant" actually means in a way a test team can be measured against.

OECD AI Principles & the UN's "Governing AI for Humanity" report

Influential international soft law — not legally binding, but widely used by governments and organizations as a shared reference point when writing their own AI strategy. They emphasize human-centric AI, ethical considerations, and international cooperation.

The EU AI Act

A landmark, legally binding regulation using a risk-based approach: AI systems are sorted into risk tiers, and higher risk means stricter obligations. Non-compliance penalties are calculated as a percentage of global company turnover — enough to get board-level attention. Many jurisdictions outside the EU take a lighter-touch approach to avoid slowing down innovation.

Underneath the law sit technical standards — these are what turn a regulation's high-level aspirations into something a test team can actually execute against. Two matter directly to this course:

  • ISO/IEC TR 29119-11 — a technical report giving detailed, practical guidance on how to test AI-based systems specifically. It's part of why this course's structure lines up with recognized testing practice rather than being invented from scratch.
  • ISO/IEC 42119 series — a family of standards under development, intended to cover multiple aspects of AI-based system testing as the field matures.
🎯
The pattern to remember Soft law (OECD/UN) sets shared values → hard law (EU AI Act and similar national regulations) makes some of those values legally enforceable, tiered by risk → technical standards (ISO/IEC TR 29119-11, ISO/IEC 42119) tell a test team concretely how to demonstrate compliance. Regulations and standards are not static — they're reviewed and updated as AI capabilities and known risks evolve, so "compliant last year" doesn't guarantee "compliant today."
