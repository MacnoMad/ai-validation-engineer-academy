1. Why almost nobody trains from scratch

Training a large model from a completely blank slate is expensive in every sense — huge amounts of labeled data, huge amounts of compute, and huge amounts of time. The practical answer the industry settled on is: reuse a pretrained model that someone else already invested that cost into, and adapt it to your specific task. There are two distinct ways to do that adaptation, and they are not interchangeable.

Fine-tuning — change the model itself

Take a pretrained neural network and continue training it, using additional data specific to your new task. This needs far less data and effort than training from scratch, because the model already "knows" a great deal of general structure — fine-tuning is really just steering that knowledge toward a narrower job.

You can fine-tune the entire network, only the layers near the output (the most common approach), or add new layers on top. After each round, ML functional performance is re-evaluated, and further fine-tuning is applied until acceptance criteria are met.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) — change what the model sees, not the model

Instead of touching the model's parameters at all, you supply it with task-specific data sources at request time. Those sources are transformed into a searchable format, the most relevant documents for the current prompt are retrieved, and they're stitched into an enhanced prompt that's handed to the LLM. More relevant context in the prompt tends to produce a more precise response — and the pretrained model itself is never modified.

⚠
Fine-tuning success depends on task similarity Adapting a cat-breed image classifier into a dog-breed classifier works well — the tasks are close. Adapting that same model to recognize spoken accents works poorly — the tasks are too different. As a validator, this is a direct testing implication: don't assume a pretrained model's known biases or vulnerabilities disappeared after fine-tuning or RAG — they typically carry over into the adapted system and need to be re-tested, not just assumed away.

2. Data preparation: where most of the real effort goes

Data preparation is widely recognized as the single most resource-intensive activity in the ML workflow — usually consuming more effort than model selection and building combined. It's tightly linked to the data pipeline: the machinery that turns raw data into a usable format for both training and later production predictions. If the data the model sees operationally drifts from the data it was trained on, ML functional performance — and any safety assumptions built on top of it — can quietly stop holding.

Data acquisition

Identifying the relevant data types you need (numerical, categorical, image, tabular, text, time series, sensor, geospatial, video, audio), then gathering it from databases, APIs, or real-time sensors. For supervised learning specifically, this includes labeling the data and verifying that the labels are accurate and consistent.

Data preprocessing

Cleaning — removing defects, duplicates, and outliers; imputing missing values (mean/median/mode); anonymizing or removing personal information. Transforming — normalizing and scaling for consistency. Augmenting — growing the sample size, adding adversarial examples for robustness, generating synthetic data. Sampling — reducing a subset to cut training time and cost.

Feature engineering

Selecting the features that actually contribute to model performance, and extracting a smaller, non-redundant, informative subset from the raw features — both in service of reducing training time and computational cost without losing signal.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

Run alongside the activities above, not after them: discovering trends, patterns, and anomalies in the data, and visualizing it with plots and charts to build real understanding of what you're about to train on.

💡
It's iterative, not a checklist Data preparation is typically manual and repeated — steps get reordered or skipped depending on the project. The one invariant: your operational data must match the training data's characteristics (distributions, feature ranges) for the model to behave as expected in the real world, even though the preparation steps themselves are often streamlined for production efficiency.
