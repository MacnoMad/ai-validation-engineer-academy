1. Dataset constraint testing

Dataset constraint testing verifies that the data in a dataset adheres to predefined rules — a logical model of what "correct" data looks like, playing the same role for an ML dataset that a database schema plays for a relational database. There are three families of constraint, distinguished by how many values they look at simultaneously.

FamilyTypeWhat it checksExample
Single-value
one attribute, one instance		 MissingIs a required value or attribute absent?An applicant record with no age field at all.

Multi-value
one attribute, many instances		 SumDoes a total across instances equal/exceed/stay under a bound?Total points awarded across an F1 season can't exceed 102.
CountDoes the count of non-null values meet a bound?At least 95% of records must have a non-null zip_code.
DuplicateAre there identical or near-identical rows beyond an allowed limit (often zero)?Two records with the exact same ID and values.
UsefulDoes an attribute actually vary, or is every value effectively unique (like an ID), giving the model no learnable pattern?A "row hash" column with a different value on every row.
OutlierAre there statistically anomalous values?An annual_income of $50,000,000 in a retail-banking dataset.
Comparison
two attributes compared		 Greater ThanIs one attribute's value reliably greater than another's?Lines of code with defects can't exceed total lines of code.
CorrelateDo two attributes' values correlate as expected?Students scoring far above the mean should also carry an 'A' grade.

3. Label correctness testing

Supervised learning is only as good as its labels — an inaccurate or inconsistent label directly undermines what the model learns to predict, and unlike a subtle feature-engineering choice, a wrong label actively teaches the wrong lesson. The syllabus names seven complementary approaches to catching this:

  • Expert review — domain experts or trained annotators manually review a sample of labelled data against their knowledge and the stated guidelines.
  • Multiple annotation — the same data points are independently labelled by several annotators and compared, essentially a form of back-to-back testing. Disagreements get investigated. The degree of agreement is the inter-annotator agreement (IAA), measured with metrics like simple percentage agreement or Cohen's Kappa. A low IAA points to defects in the labelling guidelines, ambiguous data, or inconsistent annotation.
  • Risk-based prioritization — focus expert review and multiple annotation where mislabeling is most likely (ambiguous, near-boundary cases) or where a mistake would matter most (high-impact or safety-critical data).
  • Data distribution analysis — when a comparable dataset exists, compare label distributions between them; anomalies can point to incorrect labels.
  • Automated rule-based tests — encode predefined label rules as automated checks, e.g. verifying that bounding boxes in an image dataset don't overlap or extend past the image edge.
  • Model loss analysis — data points where a trained model's predictions deviate sharply from their stated label (high loss) are candidates for mislabeling — the model is struggling to learn an assignment that may simply be wrong.
  • Model confidence score analysis — data points where the model's prediction confidence is low may be mislabeled, genuinely ambiguous, or outside the training distribution entirely.
Worked example: reading Cohen's Kappa

Two annotators independently label 50 images as "defect" or "no defect." They agree on 42 of them — 84% raw agreement. That sounds solid, but raw agreement doesn't account for the agreement you'd expect from chance alone (especially if "no defect" is the overwhelming majority class). Cohen's Kappa corrects for that: it compares the observed agreement to the agreement expected by chance, producing a score from roughly 0 (no better than chance) to 1 (perfect agreement). A high raw-agreement percentage with a mediocre Kappa is exactly the kind of signal that should send you back to tighten the labelling guidelines.

In practice these approaches are combined, not used in isolation: expert review establishes clear guidelines early, multiple-annotation IAA scores refine the process as more data is labelled, and model-based signals (loss and confidence analysis) keep surfacing potential defects even as the model itself matures.
