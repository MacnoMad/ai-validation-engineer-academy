1. AI-based systems vs. conventional systems

A conventional system is programmed: a developer writes explicit, step-by-step instructions — if, else, loops — and the same input always produces the same output. An AI-based system, particularly one built on machine learning, is trained: instead of coding the rules directly, you show it examples and it infers the patterns itself, then applies those patterns to new inputs it has never seen.

DimensionConventional systemAI-based system (typical ML)
How behavior is definedExplicit rules written by developersPatterns learned from training data
PredictabilityDeterministic — same input, same outputOften probabilistic — outputs can vary or carry a confidence level
ExplainabilityYou can read the source and trace the logicCan be a "black box," especially deep learning with millions/billions of parameters
AdaptabilityStatic until a developer manually changes the codeCan self-learn and keep improving as it sees new data
⚠
Why this matters for testing A black-box, adaptive system is exactly why AI testing needs statistical thinking (Session 11) and ongoing monitoring (Session 3, Session 19) — you can't just "read the code" to know it's correct, and correctness measured today might not hold tomorrow.

Two more terms sit inside this spectrum worth knowing precisely:

  • Frontier AI — a subset of narrow AI: today's most advanced, large-scale generative AI models. Highly capable and increasingly autonomous, but still task-specific — not general intelligence.
  • Technological singularity — the hypothetical point at which AI would cross from general AI into super AI. No system has reached general AI, so this remains theoretical.

4. Generative AI, up close

Generative AI (GenAI) systems are specialized in creating new content — text, images, video, music, or complex data — rather than only classifying or predicting. Three technologies power most of today's GenAI:

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Two neural networks compete: a generator creates fake samples, a discriminator tries to catch them. Each makes the other better until the generator's output is convincingly realistic.

Diffusion models

Start from pure noise and gradually remove it, step by step, guided by what the model learned about real data — the reverse of gradually adding noise to a real image during training.

Transformers

Use a "self-attention" mechanism to weigh how relevant every other word (or pixel) is to the one being generated — the backbone of large language models (LLMs) and increasingly of image/video models too.

Foundation & multimodal models

Most practical GenAI tools start from a large general-purpose "foundation model" that's then fine-tuned for a specific job. The field is moving toward multimodal models that handle text, image, and audio together.

The concerns this raises

GenAI's ability to create convincing synthetic content cuts both ways. As a tester, three areas belong on your radar specifically because they're testable risks, not just ethical footnotes:

  • Misuse potential — deepfakes, misinformation, and fraudulent content that erode trust in digital media. This is a big part of why red teaming (Session 12) exists as a testing discipline.
  • Workforce impact — automation of writing, design, coding, and documentation tasks raises real questions about displacement vs. augmentation.
  • Sustainability — training and running large GenAI models consumes significant compute, energy, and carbon budget, which is increasingly part of "quality" conversations at an organizational level.
